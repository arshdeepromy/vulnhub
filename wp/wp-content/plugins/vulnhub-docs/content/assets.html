The Assets screen is the register of every machine VulnHub knows about — merged from all your systems into one row each — with its owner, what has and hasn't scanned it, which systems know it, and the open exposure it carries. It is where coverage-gap drill-downs land.

What one asset row represents

A single machine may be discovered by Tenable, managed by Intune, recorded in the CMDB and seen by Defender. VulnHub merges them by matching on the strongest identifier available (Tenable UUID → Intune id → Azure AD id → serial → hostname) and remembers every contributing system. So one row can carry facts from four sources, and the Known by column tells you which (the asset page also names the system that first discovered it).

Key fields

Hostname / IP / OSIdentity and platform. The OS release is what the End-of-life widgets match against (by build number, not label).
TypeWorkstation, server, mobile, network, cloud, appliance or unknown — set by the classification rules or a connector.
Criticality & environmentHow much the machine matters, and where it sits (production/test/…). Criticality multiplies the risk score of its findings.
Owner / team / locationWho to chase. Resolved by ownership mapping — from the Intune primary user (high confidence), or the CMDB custodian (medium), or a fallback (low).
Lifecycle statusIn service, quarantine, maintenance, spare, stock, planned, retired, missing or unknown — drives whether the asset is "reportable" and whether its findings are counted or archived.
Open critical / highPre-computed roll-ups of the asset's live findings by severity, and its overall risk score.

Coverage — the blind-spot view

Every asset carries a Tenable coverage state (the Scan coverage column) and a Defender coverage state (EDR coverage), precomputed so the coverage widgets are fast. The states marked gap are the genuine blind spots that the gap-list widgets and the coverage=gap / defender=gap filters select:

Never scanned, Scan is stale, Never reported and Sensor silent are surfaced but not counted as gaps. Not a scanning target / Not an endpoint target (printers, switches, appliances) and Out of scope (retired kit) are excluded from the coverage denominator so the percentage is honest.

A third, narrower question is whether a machine carries a Tenable agent (the Agent column): Agent required (no agent, and its operating system can run one) and Agent — OS unknown (no agent, and the operating system is not one the agent support table recognises, so somebody has to look) are the gaps; Agent installed, Agent out of scope (no agent exists for that platform, or the OS is below its minimum — scan it another way) and Out of scope are not. Because Tenable keeps no history of when an agent was online, VulnHub samples it and records each change, which is what the Offline for over N days filter and the asset page's agent history read.

The two coverage percentages are not the same measure. Tenable "covered" counts any Tenable record; Defender additionally excludes device classes no agent runs on. Compare each to itself over time, not to each other.

Reachability

The threat plugin also gives each asset an internet-facing verdict with its basis — Reachable (observed evidence), Ruled out (scanned, nothing listening) or Never looked (no port scan yet). This is what the attack-path widget's edge lane depends on; the full logic is on the Routes, delivery & reachability page. A verdict a person sets by hand is preserved across rule passes. The verdict is not shown on the Assets list or the asset page: it appears on the attack-path widget's reachability strip, and as the Reachable via column on the findings list when filtered to route=edge.

Filters & drill-downs

The list's controls are Search (hostname, IP, owner, serial), Type, Team, Site (including No site recorded), Scan coverage (any gap, Known to Tenable, Dropped by Tenable — Tenable has been asked directly and no longer holds these assets while the CMDB still reports them in service — or a specific Tenable state), Tenable agent (Needs an agent, a specific agent state, or Offline for over 1, 7, 14, 30 or 90 days), Endpoint (no Defender sensor, Defender sensor, or a specific state), Known by (a source, only:<source> for single-source machines to doubt, or not:<source>), Hosting (cloud — AWS, Azure, GCP or other — or on-prem, classified exactly as the Servers by hosting environment widget does; with the AWS plugin also AWS EC2, running and AWS EC2, stopped), Lifecycle (reporting scope, everything with an owner expectation, everything including retired, outside the reporting scope, out of service only, or one status) and Owner (Any owner, Has an owner or Missing an owner). Dashboard links can also carry primary_source, operating_system, patch_group, eol and needs_user=1 (user-bound devices — workstations and mobiles — with no owner, from the Ownership gaps widget), shown as removable chips.

Columns: Host, Scan coverage (Tenable), Agent (Tenable), EDR coverage (Defender), Known by, Type, Operating system, Owner (masked until revealed), Team, Open exposure. Sort by host, type, owner or exposure; more rows load as you scroll. Under 640px the list restacks as cards.

A small icon before each hostname says where the server runs — the same marks as the Servers by hosting environment widget, and the same classification behind them, so a row and the widget cannot disagree. Cloud (AWS, Azure, GCP or other) or on-prem; the environment's name is on the tooltip and is what a screen reader announces, so the meaning never rests on the picture. A machine the rules cannot place draws nothing at all, because a shrug-shaped icon on hundreds of rows reads as a finding rather than an absence of information.

When the list is filtered by Known by (a source, or only that source), every row still shows all of its sources, but the one you filtered on is moved to the front of the Known by cell and highlighted with the accent colour and a dot, so you can scan down the column and confirm each row is what the filter says. Its tooltip notes that it is the filtered source. A not known by filter highlights nothing, because no row carries that source. Clicking any source chip filters the list to that source.

Raising a ticket for a list

Most work that comes out of this screen is not a vulnerability fix: it is "get these workstations into Tenable" or "these CMDB rows are machines that left years ago". Filter the list to exactly those machines and press Raise Jira ticket. The dialog attaches the list as a CSV (with the same column picker as Export CSV), lets you choose a request type, summary and notes, and shows everything before it sends. The ticket remembers the assets that were on screen and the filter that found them, so its page can later say which are done, which are outstanding and which were decommissioned — and Open this filter as it is today shows how the same filter reads now. See Tickets & exceptions. One ticket can hold up to 10,000 assets; a larger list is refused rather than silently truncated.

Comparing one register against another

Known by answers about one system at a time, which cannot ask the question an asset register actually needs asking: Tenable scans it, so what is it doing missing from the CMDB? Two URL filters do — has=<sources> (known by all of these) and missing=<sources> (known by none of them), each taking a comma-separated list. So ?has=tenable&missing=cmdb is every machine the scanner sees that the register has never heard of, and it behaves like any other filter: sort it, scroll it, export it, keep the link.

They arrive as a single removable chip that says what they mean in words — In Tenable, not in CMDB — and removing it clears both parameters rather than half the sentence. You rarely have to type them: the Inventory sources screen is a matrix of exactly these pairs, and every cell in it is one of these links.

One asset

?asset=<id> opens a machine's page: Ownership (owner, email, department, team, location, and what resolved it), Identity and lifecycle (FQDN, serial, hardware and support end, the systems that know it with their scan and sensor dates, Tenable agent and its online history, whether Tenable has dropped it, the system that discovered it, CMDB reference, criticality, business service, environment, patch group, last seen), Cloud (account, region, instance, network) where relevant, Open exposure by severity with tags, and its Open findings, each with a Raise Jira request button.

Decommissioning

With triage permission, tick assets and choose Mark decommissioned, Return to service, or Another status…; the same buttons sit on a single asset's page. Decommissioning takes the asset out of scan coverage and archives its open findings, so the totals stop counting risk you no longer carry. Nothing is deleted, and returning the asset to service puts every finding back as it was. You come back to the same filtered list.

The most valuable asset view is the gap list. A production server the CMDB vouches for but Tenable has never scanned is precisely the machine your dashboard can't see findings on — so the coverage-gap widgets, which land here, are often more important than the findings you already have.
