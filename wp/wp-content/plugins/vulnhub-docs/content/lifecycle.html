A finding is not a static fact — it opens when a scan first sees it, closes when a scan stops seeing it, and can reopen, be archived when its machine leaves service, or be formally excepted. This page explains those states, and the single most important filter on every screen: what counts as reportable.

The states a finding moves through

A finding is one row per asset × vulnerability × port — the working unit VulnHub counts. Its state moves as scans come in:

openCurrently detected. Live and counted.
reopenedWas fixed, then detected again by a later scan. Also live and counted.
fixedA scan that used to report it no longer does. Closed; outside the counts. The last_fixed date records when.
suppressedInformational (severity Info). Parked outside every count on purpose.
archivedIts asset left service (retired, spare, in stock…). Set aside, remembering the previous state so it can be restored exactly if the machine returns.

Only open and reopened are "live". Every headline number, roll-up, overdue count and dashboard widget counts live findings only. The row also tracks first_found, last_found, last_fixed, due_at (the remediation deadline from the team's SLA), and verification fields.

Archiving follows the asset's lifecycle

When an asset moves out of service, VulnHub archives its live findings (remembering their exact prior state); when it returns to service, they are restored precisely. This runs after every import, so re-importing data can never silently reopen findings on a machine that has been gone for months.

Lifecycle scoping — the "reportable" default

Assets carry a lifecycle_status (In service, In quarantine, In repair, Spare, In stock, Planned, Retired, Missing, Unknown). VulnHub deliberately answers three different questions with three different subsets of those statuses:

QuestionScopeIncludes
Should this asset resolve to an owner?in_service_allIn service, quarantine, maintenance, unknown
Should Tenable have scanned it?coverage scopeIn service, unknown (editable)
Should it appear in a reported number?reportable (default)In service, unknown

The findings and asset screens offer three scopes, and reportable is the default:

reportableLive and unknown-status machines someone can actually act on — minus off-network (quarantine/maintenance) and un-issued kit (spare/stock/planned/retired). This is what a reported figure is about.
in_service_allEverything currently in service, including quarantine and maintenance.
allNo lifecycle filter at all — every asset, whatever its status.

Why unknown is included in "reportable". It is tempting to report only confirmed in-service machines. But on a real estate the vast majority of open findings sit on assets whose lifecycle status simply hasn't been set yet — excluding unknown would report a tiny fraction of reality and hide almost everything. So "reportable" errs toward showing the work, while still dropping machines that are genuinely off-network or not yet issued.

Exceptions — formally accepting a risk

An exception is how a finding is removed from the counts on purpose, with an audit trail. It is not "ignore" — it is a recorded decision with a reason, a justification an auditor will read, and an expiry date.

Anatomy of an exception

Scopefinding (one finding on one asset), asset (all findings on one asset), vuln (one vulnerability across all assets), or team (one vulnerability across one team).
Reasonfalse positive, compensating control, no fix available, business impact, end of life, not exploitable, accepted risk, or other.
Statusdraft → pending → approved (or rejected); later expired or revoked.
JustificationRequired, at least 20 characters — "the record an auditor will read".
ExpiryMandatory and in the future. An exception is a temporary, reviewed decision, never permanent.

What approval does to the numbers

On approval, the exception stamps its id onto every matching open/reopened finding, and those findings immediately drop out of every live count (they now have a non-zero exception_id). A separate "excepted" metric counts them so the accepted risk is never invisible. Asset roll-ups are recalculated on approval, revoke and expiry, so severity totals move the moment the decision changes.

Each night, approved exceptions past their expiry date flip to expired and their findings return to the open counts (only approved exceptions are honoured). Revoking an exception explicitly clears the stamp from its findings straight away. Reference numbers are auto-assigned (EXC-0001, …) so every decision is citable.

See Tickets & exceptions for the day-to-day workflow, and The Vulnerabilities list for how to filter to (or away from) excepted findings.

End of support — the EOL plan screen

A finding's lifecycle is one thing; a machine's support lifecycle is another. The lifecycle table tells you a server runs a release its vendor stopped patching. It cannot tell you whether anyone is dealing with that — which project owns it, and by when. That answer comes from the end-of-support retirement programme, imported from its reconciliation workbook under Administration → Data → EOS remediation plan, and shown on the EOL plan screen.

This is imported data, not live scanner data. Every screen states the date it was last uploaded, because a plan is only as current as the last upload, and a security screen that hides its own age is worse than one that admits it.

Covered, or not

Everything on that screen — and the green and red bands on the dashboard's Platforms past end of life widget — reduces to one question:

CoveredRemediated already, or a named project whose date is still ahead of it. Drawn green.
OverdueA project exists, but its quarter has been and gone without the server being remediated. Counted with the red, and labelled so you can tell it apart from work nobody has scheduled.
Not coveredNo plan, no date (blank or TBD), a visibility gap the scanners cannot verify, or a machine past end of life the programme has never assessed at all. Drawn red.

Coverage is worked out when the screen is read, never stored. A plan whose quarter ends tonight reads overdue tomorrow morning without anybody re-importing anything — which is the point of tracking it here rather than in the spreadsheet.

Quarters follow a financial year starting 1 April, so FY26 runs 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026 and "overdue" is measured against the last day of the quarter named. If your fiscal calendar differs, that is a one-line change — ask whoever maintains the platform.

What the screen gives you

Tiles for in-the-programme / covered / overdue / not covered (each one a filter), a forward workload chart of what still needs action by quarter, and the server table: treatment plan, timeframe with its due date, programme state, RAG, owner team, site, open critical and high findings, and last scan. Filter by coverage, state, timeframe, environment tier, RAG or project alongside the portal's own search, team and site, then Export CSV — which exports everything matching the filter, not just the page you can see.

Machines the programme has never assessed are included deliberately. They are past end of life and written down nowhere else, which makes them the most interesting rows on the screen. Programme rows with no matching asset are kept too, marked not in inventory: the programme knowing about a server the inventory does not is itself worth seeing.
