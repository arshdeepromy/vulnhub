The dashboard is a board of widgets you can rearrange. Each widget answers one question and — almost always — every number on it is a link that opens the exact list of findings or assets it counted. This page explains all 39 widgets (36 in core, plus three added by the threat, departments and hosting plugins): what each shows, what its figures mean, how the data is filtered, and where clicking takes you.

How the board works. Drag the handle on any widget to rearrange (or focus the handle and use the arrow keys); use Customise to add or remove widgets from the picker or Reset to default. Customise needs JavaScript. Your layout is saved to your account. Each widget's header has an export menu — CSV / Copy as TSV when the widget has tabular data, and PNG / SVG when it draws a chart. Widgets are grouped in the picker as Exposure, End of life, Tenable coverage, Defender coverage, Ownership and Remediation.

Two things to know before reading numbers. (1) Tenable coverage % and Defender coverage % are not comparable — they use different denominators (Defender additionally excludes device classes no agent runs on). That is why they are in separate groups. (2) A few widgets (Findings by age, Impact funnel, Impact by OS, Impact by family, Most widespread vulnerabilities, Most exposed assets, Exposure by team) are not filtered to the reporting scope, so their totals can be larger than the scoped headline figures. Everywhere else, "reportable" scope applies (see Finding lifecycle & scoping).

The attack-path widget: How an attacker gets in

This is the widget added by the threat plugin and pinned directly under the headline. It is the single most important picture on the board, so it has its own concept page; in brief:

  • It shows only open findings that have a working exploit, sorted into the route an attacker would take.
  • edge — reached straight from the internet (no account, no click) on an internet-facing machine. This should read zero.
  • user — delivered to a person; the three doors (email / website / download) sum to this lane.
  • inside — only usable after a foothold, plus edge findings on machines the internet cannot currently reach.
  • Every number links into the findings list filtered to exactly that lane or door (route=…, delivery=…, poc=1). The reachability strip below it reports what is Reachable, Never looked and Ruled out.

Group: Exposure

Headline figures

Four big KPI tiles. Critical open = open, non-excepted critical findings on in-scope assets (with the high count beside it). Past SLA = open findings past their due date. Not scanned by Tenable = in-scope assets with a Tenable coverage gap (with coverage % beside it). Closed but still detected = tickets Jira marked done that Tenable still sees. Critical open links to the open criticals, Past SLA to overdue=1, Not scanned to assets?coverage=gap&life=reportable, and Closed but still detected to the (unfiltered) Tickets screen. The first two tiles carry a 30-day sparkline.

The Past SLA tile's sparkline plots the overdue trend from the nightly snapshot. Snapshots only began recording recently, so the line is short until 30 days of history accumulate.

Open findings over time (trend)

A four-line chart (Critical/High/Medium/Low) of open findings per day over the last 30 days. It reads a nightly snapshot table, not live data, so it shows the direction of travel — is exposure rising or falling.

Findings by severity

A donut of currently-open, non-excepted findings split across the five severities; the centre is the total open. The proportional shape of what is open right now.

Findings by age

A severity-stacked bar per age band (0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–180, 180+ days, plus Unknown), aged by when each finding was first discovered. A large 180+ critical segment means long-standing unfixed criticals. (Not scope-filtered.)

Vulnerabilities by severity and age

A matrix: rows are severities, columns are three age bands (Under 30 / 30–90 / Over 90 days) plus a Movement column. Each cell's big number is the count of distinct vulnerabilities whose oldest open instance falls in that band ("counted once"); the sub-line is how many asset findings those represent. The Movement pill is vulnerabilities that resurfaced minus those fixed (↓ green = improving, ↑ red = worse). Every cell links to the findings list filtered by that severity and age.

Impact funnel

A four-stage funnel: Open findings → Critical or high → With a public exploit (the vuln has exploit_available) → And nobody has a ticket. The last stage is the unmanaged-risk number that matters most. (Not scope-filtered.)

Most widespread vulnerabilities

A bar chart of the critical, high and medium vulnerabilities affecting the most assets, largest first. "Fix these once and the number moves." (Filters on the definition's severity; not scope-filtered.)

Each bar opens that vulnerability's detail page (?vuln=<id>) with its affected assets.

Most exposed assets

The machines with the highest stored risk score, with the owner (person, else team, else "Unassigned") and their open critical/high counts — who to chase first. Each hostname links to the single-asset view.

Impact by operating system / Impact by product family

Two "where does the work sit" views. By OS is a donut of open findings weighted by the operating system of the affected machine; by family is a bar chart of open findings by Tenable plugin family (e.g. "Windows", "Web Servers"). Both are finding-weighted, so a machine with many findings dominates. (Not scope-filtered; both are off the default board but in the picker.)

Vulnerable software in Downloads folders

Open findings whose vulnerable file sits in a user's Downloads folder — software nobody patches and no packaging system tracks — split into Windows / Linux / macOS tiles, in reporting scope. A separate footnote counts the informational file listings Tenable catalogued (deliberately kept out of the vulnerability count). Tiles link to the filtered findings.

Patch availability by severity

Per severity, a bar split into Patch available (green — the vendor has a fix, so it is schedulable) and No patch available (red — nobody has a fix, so it needs a decision, not a patch window). The scale is per-row so you see the proportion fixable at each severity. Each half links to that severity + patch-state in the findings list.

Exposure by product

The flagship "what software carries the risk" list: each row is a product (app, library or OS package), showing the assets affected (machines to touch) and total findings, with a proportional bar and a kind badge (library / app / OS). A vulnerable library is regrouped under the application that ships it. Fix the top rows and the number moves fastest. Names link to the product's findings; "View all products" opens the full list. See Products & vendors.

Group: End of life

These match your estate against published end-of-support dates. Status buckets: Out of support (past the date), Support ends within six months, Supported, and No published date. The wording is "out of support", not "past end of life", because these are release branches, not dead products.

Platforms past end of life

Assets grouped by the OS release they run, matched on build number rather than what the inventory label claims (so a machine labelled "Windows 11" actually running an old build is placed correctly, or left "unknown" rather than guessed). Four tiles give the estate split; the table adds "Mainstream ends" (a different promise) and days remaining.

Each row is named for the release, not the product — Red Hat Enterprise Linux 6, not three rows all reading "Red Hat Enterprise Linux" with the 6, the 5 and the 7 demoted to the grey line below. A Windows row keeps its build, because the build is what this product matches on and what settles an argument about which release a machine is really running. The vendor's mark sits beside the name, the same artwork the assets list draws, so the OS column and this chart name a platform the same way; a family with no logo shows nothing rather than a placeholder.

Each bar is split in two: green for the machines covered by a remediation project with a date still ahead of them, red for those not covered — no plan, no date, never assessed, or a planned date that has already passed. The red band's tooltip says how many of it are overdue rather than never-planned. Selecting either half opens the EOL plan screen filtered to exactly those servers, so the number on the bar and the list behind it always agree.

The whole numbers are clickable too. The total beside a bar opens that release's servers with no coverage filter — both halves at once. Past end of life and Ends within 6 months open the same screen restricted to the machines that tile counts. Supported and Release unknown deliberately do not link: the remediation programme has nothing to say about them. Neither does a tile reading zero, because an empty list is a worse answer than the zero already on the screen.

The split needs the remediation programme imported (Administration → Data → EOS remediation plan). Without it the bars stay coloured by support status, exactly as before — nothing breaks, you simply get no answer about who is dealing with what.

Software on unsupported releases

Installed software (from each asset's software inventory) on a release the vendor no longer patches, counted once per machine, with the supported release to move to. (Off the default board; in the picker. Rows are not clickable here.)

Hardware support

A single stacked bar of warranty status from the CMDB's support-end date: Out of hardware support / Expires within 90 days / Under support / No date recorded. This is a warranty date, not a security one — an out-of-warranty machine still gets OS updates — which is why it is never merged into platform EOL. (No CSV; off the default board.)

Group: Tenable coverage

What "covered" and "gap" mean here. "Covered" = Tenable has any record of the asset (even a stale or never-scanned one). A "gap" is only Not in Tenable or No recent contact — stale/never-scanned are shown as "unscanned" but are not gaps. The denominator excludes retired kit and things nobody scans (printers, switches).

Tenable coverage

A donut with the headline coverage percentage in the centre — how much of the in-scope estate Tenable has a record of. The sub-line names the assets excluded from the figure (by lifecycle status, and un-scannable kit) and links to them.

Tenable coverage by device type / by site / by source system

The same coverage bar, broken down three ways, worst coverage first: by device type, by site/location (the "Unclassified"/unplaced row usually holds the most gaps), and by source system (of the machines this system says exist, how many Tenable knows — plus a "claimed by one system alone" flag for machines to doubt). Every bar links to those gap assets. (By device type and by source system are on the default board; by site is in the picker.)

Tenable coverage of the CMDB

A meter + gap tile focused on the CMDB register specifically: of the assets the CMDB says exist, how many Tenable has a record of. The gap tile links to the CMDB assets Tenable is missing.

Tenable gap list / Tenable gaps in the CMDB register

The actionable tables: assets something knows about that Tenable does not, with why it is a gap, which systems know it, the owner, and last scan. The CMDB variant (on the default board) is every-row-vouched — each has a CI, owner and service, so it is directly actionable. Hostnames link to the asset; the footer opens the full gap list.

Group: Defender coverage

The same shape as Tenable coverage, but for the Microsoft Defender sensor, reading the precomputed defender_coverage_state. Different denominator: in-scope here additionally excludes appliances, network gear and mobiles, and devices Defender flags as unsupported — so do not compare this percentage with Tenable's.

Defender coverage

A donut with the centre percentage of in-scope assets that have a Defender sensor, and a footnote listing what is excluded (including "servers and workstations Defender cannot onboard" — the reason its denominator differs from Tenable's).

Defender coverage by device type / by source system, and of the CMDB

Worst-first breakdowns by device type and source system, and a meter focused on the CMDB register — "onboarded" counts as covered, "onboarded but sensor quiet" is surfaced separately. Bars and tiles link to the assets with no sensor.

Defender gap list / Defender gaps in the CMDB register

Tables of in-scope assets with no reporting Defender sensor, ordered by criticality, with why it is a gap, who knows it, the owner and the last sensor report. The CMDB variant is near the end of the default board; the general list is in the picker.

Group: Ownership

Exposure by team

A severity-stacked bar per team (top 8 by critical→high→total) — who currently carries the open findings. "Unassigned" is findings on assets with no team. The team label links to that team's findings; each severity segment links to that severity within the team.

Vulnerabilities by department

The same shape as Exposure by team, but grouped by the department that owns the device (finding → asset → owner → department), largest first. Selecting a bar opens that department's findings; a coloured segment opens it filtered to one severity. Departments come from an Entra export you upload under Admin → Departments — see Departments. The widget is always on the default board (after Exposure by team); until departments are recorded it says so and points to Admin → Departments. Its "all departments" link opens a dedicated, hidden departments page.

Ownership gaps

Workstations and mobiles, in scope, with no owner person — highest-risk first, so you know who has nobody to chase. Hostnames link to the asset; the footer opens the full "needs a user" list.

Estate by device type

A donut of the in-scope fleet by device type — a population breakdown, not a risk figure. (In the picker.)

Group: Remediation

Remediation health

Three meters: Findings within SLA (open findings not past their due date), Assets with an owner or team (in-scope assets that resolve to a person or team), and Estate scanned by Tenable (coverage). One glance at whether remediation is keeping up.

Closure verification

A donut of what happened when VulnHub re-checked tickets Jira called "done": Verified fixed (green — a rescan confirmed it gone), Still detected (red — a false closure), Cannot verify (amber), Not yet checked (grey). "A ticket marked done is a claim; this is what re-checking found."

Ticket flow

A donut of remediation tickets by status category — To do / In progress / Done / Unknown — with the total in the centre.

Exceptions expiring

Approved risk acceptances with an expiry date, soonest first. When one lapses its findings stop being excepted and re-enter the open counts — so this is your review queue. References link to the exceptions list. (In the picker.)

Servers by hosting environment

Added by the hosting plugin (Exposure group, full width, at the end of the default board). Servers are classified as cloud (AWS / Azure / GCP / other cloud) or on-prem, or left unclassified, from cloud instance ids, the location register and hostname naming. Each environment shows its server count, the Linux / Windows split and its open findings by severity, and links to its findings (hosting=…).

The default board

A new user's board, in order: Headline figures, How an attacker gets in, Vulnerabilities by severity and age, Exposure by product, Open findings over time, Findings by severity, Patch availability, Vulnerable software in Downloads folders, Platforms past end of life, Tenable coverage, Tenable coverage of the CMDB, Defender coverage of the CMDB, Tenable coverage by device type, Tenable coverage by source system, Defender coverage, Defender coverage by device type, Findings by age, Impact funnel, Exposure by team, Vulnerabilities by department, Ownership gaps, Most widespread vulnerabilities, Most exposed assets, Remediation health, Closure verification, Ticket flow, Tenable gaps in the CMDB register, Defender gaps in the CMDB register, Tenable gap list, Servers by hosting environment. Everything else is in the picker.

How the numbers stay fresh

Each widget's rendered HTML is cached for its host and language, fresh for 15 minutes and served stale for up to 6 hours while a background refresh runs, so a page load rarely waits on the database. A finished sync marks stale only the widgets that read the data it moved; saving Settings, a decommission, a coverage recalculation or an import marks everything stale. An hourly cron re-warms the board as a safety net. There is no manual refresh button — an administrator can run wp cache flush. See Building a widget for the mechanics.
