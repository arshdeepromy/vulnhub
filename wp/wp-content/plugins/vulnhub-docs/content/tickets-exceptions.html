Two workflows turn findings into decisions: tickets (do the work) and exceptions (formally accept the risk). Both attach to findings and both move the numbers.

Tickets — driving remediation

A ticket is a piece of work tracked in Jira. There are two kinds. A vulnerability ticket is linked to one or more findings and asks for them to be fixed. An asset request is raised from a filtered list on Assets & owners — "get these machines into Tenable", "these CMDB rows have left" — and remembers exactly which assets were on screen, so its page can later say which are done, which are outstanding and which stopped mattering because they were decommissioned. VulnHub keeps both in step with Jira.

How tickets flow

RaiseFrom a finding or a selection of findings, VulnHub drafts a Jira issue (routed by Jira routing to the right service desk and team), shows you everything it will send — with the finding list attached as a CSV — and creates it only when you press Send to Jira. The findings then show their ticket. An asset request is raised the same way from Raise Jira ticket on the assets list; if you raised it in Jira by hand, I already raised it in JSM records its key against the list without sending anything.
SyncTicket status is pulled back from Jira. The Ticket flow widget shows the split by status category — To do / In progress / Done.
ReopenIf a still-detected issue was closed, the reopener flags it — a closed ticket is a claim, not proof.
VerifyWhen a ticket closes, VulnHub waits a settling period (Settings → Closure verification delay, default 24 hours, so the scanner has time to run again), then re-checks each covered finding against current Tenable data through a vulnerability export narrowed to those plugins. It does not trigger a scan itself. You can also press Verify on a ticket (or Verify all tickets) at any time: it reads the ticket's status, assignee and latest comment from Jira and re-checks its findings against what Tenable already holds. Launching a rescan from Verify is off unless it is switched on in the Tenable settings, and even then servers are never rescanned from here. Without anyone pressing it, each ticket is also checked at 10:00 the morning after its assets' scheduled Tenable scan, and on its due date. Tickets already verified fixed are skipped.

Verification — "closed" vs "actually fixed"

This is the point of the Closure verification widget. Each ticket carries a verification state:

Awaiting verificationClosed in Jira; not yet re-checked. (The widget labels this Not yet checked.)
Verified fixedTenable now reports every covered finding fixed, or no longer reports it on an asset that has since been rescanned. The good outcome.
Still detectedTenable still sees it — a false closure. The finding goes back to reopened, and the Jira issue is commented on and reopened.
Cannot verifyThe asset has not been rescanned since the ticket closed, so remediation is unproven either way.
Not requiredNo verification applies to this ticket (for example, it is still open).

The dashboard's "Closed but still detected" headline tile counts the still detected tickets — the closures you cannot trust. Assets set aside on a ticket (below) are left out of its verdict, and the verdict says how many findings that was.

The Tickets screen

The screen opens with two reports before the list. Raised vs not raised shows, per severity, how many open findings on reported assets are already on a ticket and how many are not, with Severity, Patch and Operating-system controls; every segment opens exactly those findings, and a "not raised" number is the quickest way to raise the next batch. The Ticket report measures SLA outcome (Met SLA, On track, Closed late, Overdue, No due date), time to resolve and raised vs resolved over the last 12 weeks; its numbers filter the list below. Both export CSV.

Under All tickets sit four tiles (Open, Closed, Awaiting verification, Closed but still detected), a For you strip, Open / Closed / All tabs (Open is the default), filters for Search (key or summary), Status (To do / In progress / Done), Request type and Verification, and a table of Key, Type, Summary, Status, Assigned to, Latest comment, Covers, Verification and Last check. Where a service desk routes by team rather than by person, Assigned to names the team and says so.

For you — what is waiting on you

The For you strip counts open tickets that need something from the person looking, and each count filters the list:

Mentioned youSomebody @mentioned your Jira account, or wrote your name, in a comment, and you have not commented since.
Waiting on your replySomebody has mentioned you since your last comment. Merely being the newest commenter on a ticket you raised is not enough — routing notes and status updates are not asking you anything. Your next comment clears it.
Assigned to youThe Jira assignee is your account.
Overdue, current list sentPast its due date and you have already sent the current list, so the ticket is the other team's to finish and yours to chase. This one is highlighted.
OverduePast its due date, but the current list has not been sent — sending it is the next step.

Nothing here calls Jira: the comments were summarised when the ticket was last read. If no Jira account matches your email and name, the strip says so rather than guessing.

Working a ticket without opening Jira

ViewRead the ticket's comments and reply — as an Internal note (only agents see it) or a Reply to customer.
Change statusMove the ticket to any status its Jira workflow offers from where it is now, with an optional comment posted alongside.
VerifyRe-check the ticket now (see above). The Last check column shows when it was last checked and what it found, and when the next automatic check is due.

A ticket's own page shows its progress bar (findings or assets done out of the total), its findings (filter by Open, Reopened, Fixed, Resolved: out of service or Set aside; group them by asset; pick your columns, which are remembered for you on every ticket) and a By department tab. Send an updated list to Jira attaches today's list of what is still open and leaves an internal note saying so.

Setting assets aside

On a ticket's findings grouped by asset, select machines and press Set aside for this ticket, choosing Resolved (dealt with another way) or Out of scope (not this ticket's business). Their findings stop counting as outstanding on this ticket only — the same machine stays in scope on every other ticket and everywhere else — and closure verification skips them. Take back reverses it.

SLAs

SLA targets are per severity (by default: Critical 7 days, High 30, Medium 90, Low 180). Settings → SLA source decides whether each team's own targets apply (set under Ownership → Teams, the default) or one organisation-wide set. A finding's due date is set from those targets, and the "Past SLA" / "Findings within SLA" figures are computed against it. An asset request is due a fixed number of days after it is raised (30 by default).

Automations

Ticket creation can be automated: rules match on finding attributes and raise (grouped) tickets on events, with throttles and per-run caps, so a flood of new findings doesn't create a flood of tickets. Automations are configured in the admin area.

Exceptions — accepting a risk on purpose

Sometimes the right answer is not "fix it" but "accept it, with a reason, for now". An exception records that decision and removes the affected findings from the live counts — visibly, with an audit trail, never silently.

Raising one

From a finding, request an exception. You choose:

ScopeThis finding (one finding on one asset), this asset (all its findings), this vulnerability (across all assets), a team (one vulnerability across a team), or a bundling application (every bundled library or file inside one application, now and as found — offered only when the finding is such a component, and from the Request exception control on a product in the By product tab).
ReasonFalse positive, compensating control, no fix available, business impact, end of life, not exploitable, accepted risk, or other.
JustificationRequired, and at least 20 characters — the record an auditor will read.
ExpiryRequired by the form, which defaults it to 90 days out; the server rejects a date in the past. Exceptions are temporary, reviewed decisions.

The Except button on a findings row opens the request form inside the portal's Exception register, and the register's Ref column opens an exception for review there too.

The Exception register

Tiles for Awaiting approval, Active, Findings suppressed (still tracked, not counted as open) and Expiring in 30 days; a Status filter (Draft, Pending approval, Approved, Rejected, Expired, Revoked); and a table of Ref, Title, Reason, Findings, Status, Requested by and Expires. Find a finding to except takes a requester to the Vulnerabilities list.

The lifecycle of an exception

An exception moves draft → pending → approved (or rejected). Approval requires the approver capability. On approval it stamps the matching open findings, which immediately drop out of the live counts (a separate "excepted" figure keeps them visible). New findings that match an active exception's scope — the next scan's copy of the same vulnerability, say — are covered too, after every sync and every night. Each night, approved exceptions past their expiry flip to expired and their findings return to the counts — which is what the Exceptions expiring widget is warning you about. Revoking one clears its findings straight away. Every exception carries a reference (EXC-0001, …) so decisions are citable.

Why this matters for the numbers. An excepted finding is not deleted and not ignored — it is accounted for. That is the difference between a dashboard you can defend in an audit and one you can't. See Finding lifecycle & scoping for exactly how exceptions interact with counts.
